# 33.1 Electromagnetic Waves
- An EM wave consists of oscillating E and B fields
	- Maxwell's Rainbow!
- The various possible frequencies of EM waves form a spectrum, a small part of which is visible light. 
- AN EM wave traveling along an x-axis has an E field and B field with magnitudes that depend on $x$ and $t$: $$\begin{aligned} E &= E_m\sin(kx-\omega t) \\ & \\B &= B_m\sin(kx-\omega t) \end{aligned}$$where  $E_m$ and $B_m$ are the amplitudes of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$. ==The electric field induces the magnetic field and vice versa.==
	- B field varies sinusoidally --> Faraday's Law of Induction --> induces perpendicular E field, which varies sinusoidally --> Maxwell's Law of Induction --> perpendicular B field, which varies sinusoidally
	- The two fields continuously create each other w/ induction
- The speed of any EM wave in vacuum is $c$, which can be written as $$c = \frac{E}{B}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}$$where $E$ and $B$ are the simultaneous magnitudes of the fields. 
- Key Features
	1. The E and B fields are always perp. to the direction in which the wave is traveling (a transverse wave)
	2. The E field is always perpendicular to the B field
	3. The cross product $\vec E \times \vec B$ always gives the direction in which the wave travels
	4. The fields always vary sinusoidally; the fields vary with the same frequency and in phase with each other
- Representation of EM waves
	- Ray: directed line showing the wave's direction of travel
		- Beam: waves traveling in approximately the same direction (can be represented w/ a ray)
	- Wavefront: imaginary surface over which the wave has the same magnitude of E field
- EM waves require no medium for travel
- Light has the same speed regardless of frame of reference
# 33.2 Energy Transport and the Poynting Vector
- An EM wave transports energy!
- The rate per unit area at which energy is transported via an EM wave is given by the Poynting vector $\vec S$: $$\vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec E \times \vec B=\frac{1}{c\mu_0}E^2$$the direction of S (and thus of the wave's travel and the energy transport) is perpendicular to both E and B
	- E and B are closely coupled so you can substitute
- The time-averaged rate per unit area at which energy is transported is $S_{avg}$, which is called the intensity $I$ of the wave $$I = \frac{1}{c\mu_0}E^2_{rms}$$in which $E_{rms}=E_m/\sqrt{2}$ 
- A point source of the EM waves emits the waves isotropically--that is, which equal intensity in all directions. The intensity of the waves t distance $r$ from a point source of power $P_s$ is $$I = \frac{P_s}{4\pi r^2}$$
# 33.3 Radiation Pressure
- When a surface intercepts EM radiation, a force and a pressure are exerted on the surface
- If the radiation is totally absorbed by the surface, the force is $$F = \frac{IA}{c}$$in which $I$ is the intensity of the radiation and $A$ is the area of the surface perp. to the path of radiation
- If the radiation is totally reflected along its orig. path, the force is $$F = \frac{2IA}{c}$$
- The radiation pression $p_r$ is the force per unit area 
	- total absorption $p_r = \frac{I}{c}$
	- total reflection back along path $p_r = \frac{2I}{c}$ 
- EM waves have linear moment and thus can exert a pressure on an object when shining on it
# 33.4 Polarization
- EM waves are polarized if their E field vectors are all in a single plane, called the pane of oscillation. Light waves from common sources are not polarized (unpolarized/polarized randomly)
- When a polarizing sheet is placed in the path of light, only E field components || to the sheet's polarizing direction are transmitted by the sheet; components perpendicular to the polarizing direction are absorbed. The light that emerges from a polarizing sheet is polarized paralell to the polarizing direction fo the sehet. 
- If the original light is initially unpolarized, the transmitted intensity $I$ is half the original intensity $$I = \frac{1}{2}I_0$$
- If the original light is initially polarized, the transmitted intensity depends on the angle between the polaristion direction of the orig. light and the polarizing direction of the sheet $$I = I_0 \cos^2\theta$$
# 33.5 Reflection and Refraction
- Geometric optics is an approximate treatment of light in which light waves are represented as straight-line rays
- When a light ray encounters a boundary between 2 transparent media, a reflected ray and a refracted ray generally appear. Both rays remain in the plane of incidence. The angle of reflection is equal to the angle of incidence, and the angle of refraction is related to the angle of incidence by Snell's Law $$n_2\sin\theta_2=n_1\sin\theta_1$$where $n_1$ and $n_2$ are the indexes of refraction of the media in which the incident and refracted rays travel

# 33.6 Total Internal Reflection
- A wave encountering a boundary across which the index of refraction decreases will experiences TIR if the angle of incidence exceeds a critical angle $\theta_c$, where $$\theta_c=\sin^{-1}\frac{n_2}{n1}$$
# 33.7 Polarization by Reflection
- A reflected wave will be fully polarized, with its $\vec E$ vectors perp. to the plane of incidence, if it strikes a bounary at the Brewster angle $$\theta_B=\tan^{-1}\frac{n_2}{n_1}$$
