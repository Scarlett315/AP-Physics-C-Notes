# 13.1 Newton's Law of Gravitation
- Any particle in the universe attracts any other particle with a gravitational force $$F=G\frac{m_1m_2}{r^2} \quad \text{(Newton's Law of Gravitation)}$$ or $$\vec{F} = G\frac{m_1m_2}{r^2}\hat{r}$$
	- where $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the masses of the particles, _r_ is their separation, and G is the gravitational constant (= $6.67 \times 10^-11 \ \ N\cdot m^2/kg^2$ )
- The gravitational force between extended bodies is found by adding (integrating) the individual forces on individual particles within the bodies. However, if either of the bodies is a uniform spherical shell (Newton's shell theorem) or a spherically symmetric solid (many shells inside each other), the net gravitational force it exerts on an external object may be computed as if all of the mass of the shell or body were located at its center. 
- The gravitation of two bodies is a third-force law pair
# 13.2 Gravitation and the Principle of Superposition
- Superposition: general principle that says a net effect is the sum of the individual effects
- Gravitational forces obey the principle of superposition, i.e. if _n_ particles interact, the net force on a particle (particle 1) is the sum of the forces on it from all the other particles taken one at a time: $$\vec{F}_{1, net} = \sum^n_{i=2}\vec{F}_1i$$ in which the sum is a vector sum of the forces $\vec{F_{1i}}$ on particle 1 from particles 2, 3, ...n.
- The gravitational force on a particle from an extended body is found by first dividing the body into nits of differential mass _dm_, each of which produces a differential force $d\vec{F}$ on the particle, and then integrating over all those units to find the sum of those forces: $$\vec{F_1} = \int d\vec{F}$$
# 13.3 Gravitation Near Earth's Surface
- The gravitational acceleration $a_g$ of a particle of mass _m_ is due solely to the gravitational force acting on it. When the particle is at distance _r_ from the center of a uniform, spherical body of mass _M_, the magnitude _F_ of the grav. force on the particle is given by $F = ma_g$. Thus, $$a_g = \frac{GM}{r^2}$$
- Because Earth's mass is not distributed uniformly, because the planet is not perfectly spherical, and because it rotates, the actual free-fall acceleration $\vec{g}$ differs slightly from the gravitational acceleration, and the particle's weight (mg) differs from the magnitude of the grav. force on it
# 13.4 Gravitation Inside Earth
- A uniform shell of matter exerts no net gravitational force on a particle located inside it
- The gravitational force $\vec{F}$ on a particle inside a uniform solid sphere at a distance r from the center is due only to mass $M_{ins}$ in an "inside sphere" with that radius _r_: $$M_{ins} = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho = \frac{M}{R^3}r^3$$ where $\rho$ is the solid sphere's density, _R_ is its radius, and M its mass. 
- We can assign this inside mass to be that of a particle at the center of the solid sphere and then apply Newton's law of gravitation for particles. We find that the magnitude of the force acting on mass _m_ is $$F = \frac{GmM}{R^3}r$$
# 13.5 Gravitational Potential Energy

- The gravitational potential energy _U(r)_ of a system of two particles of mass M and m and separated by a dist. r, is the negative of the work that would be done by the gravitational force of either particle acting on the other if the separation between the particles were changed from infinite to r. This energy is $$ U = -\frac{GMm}{r} \quad \text{(gravitational PE)}$$
- If a system contains more than two particles, its total GPE is the sum of the terms representing the potential energies of the pairs. As an example, for three particles: $$U = -(\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r_{12}}+\frac{Gm_1m_3}{r_{13}}+\frac{Gm_2m_3}{r_{23}})$$
- An object will escape the gravitational pull of an astronomical body of mass _M_ and radius _R_ (i.e. it will reach an infinite distance) if the object's speed near the body's surface is at least equal to the escape speed, given by $$v=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}$$
	- b/c at infinity, KE + U = 0 & so at the beginning the sum of energies must have been infinity --> $K + U = \frac{1}{2}mv^2+(-\frac{GMm}{r})=0$ 
# 13.6 Planets and Satellites: Kepler's Laws
- The motion of satellites, both natural and artificial, is governed by Kepler's laws:
	1. The law of orbits: All planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus
	2. The law of areas: A line joining any planet to the sun sweeps out equal areas in equal time intervals (= conservation of angular momentum)
	3. The law of periods: The square of the period T of any planet is proportional to the cube of the semimajor axis a of its orbit. For circular orbits with radius r, $$T^2=(\frac{4\pi^2}{GM})r^3$$ where M is the mass of the attracting body. For elliptical orbits, the semimajor axis a is substituted for r. 
# 13.7 Satellites: Orbits, and Energy
- When a planet or satellite with mass _m_ moves in a circular orbit with radius _r_, its U and KE are given by $$U=-\frac{GMm}{r} \quad \text{and} \quad KE=\frac{GMm}{2r}\quad; \quad K=\frac{-U}{2}$$
- The mechanical energy E = K + U is then $$E=-\frac{GMm}{2r}$$
- For an elliptical orbit of semimajor axis a, $$E=\frac{-GMm}{2a}$$
# 13.8 Einstein and Gravitation
- Einstein pointed out that gravitation and acceleration are equivalent. This principle of equivalence led him to a theory of gravitation (general theory of relativity) that explains gravitational effects in terms of a curvature of space
