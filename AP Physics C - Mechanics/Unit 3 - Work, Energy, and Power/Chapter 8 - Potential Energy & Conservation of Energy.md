# 8.1 Potential Energy
- A force is **conservative** if the net work it does on a particle moving around any closed path, from an initial point and then back to that point is zero; i.e. the net work does not depend on the path taken by the particle
	- Gravitational & spring -- conservative, kinetic friction -- nonconservative
- **Potential Energy**: Energy associated with the configuration of a system in which a conservative force acts
	- When the conservative force does work _W_ on a particle, the change $\Delta U$ in the potential energy of the system is $$\Delta U = -W$$
	- If the particle moves from point $x_i$ to $x_f$, the change in $\Delta U$ of the system is: $$\Delta U = -\int^{x_f}_{x_i}F(x)dx$$
- The potential energy associated with a system consisting of Earth and a nearby particle is gravitational potential energy. If the particle moves from height $y_i$ to $y_f$, the change in the GPE of the particle-earth system is $$\Delta U=mg(y_f-y_i)=mg\Delta y$$
	- If the ref. point of the particle is set $y_i = 0$ w/ GPE $U_i = 0$, $$U(y)=mgy$$
	- GPE associated w/ a particle-Earth system depends only on the vertical position/height of the particle relative to the ref. position
- For a spring that exerts a spring force _F = -kx_ when its free end has displacement _x_, the EPE is: $$U(x)=\frac{1}{2}kx^2$$
	- where the reference configuration has the spring at its relaxed length, at which x = 0 and U = 0
	- More generally $$\Delta U=\frac{1}{2}kx_f^2-\frac{1}{2}kx_i^2$$
# 8.2 Conservation of Mechanical Energy
- The mechanical energy $E_{mec}$ of a system is the sum of its KE and U: $$E_{mec} = K + U$$
- An isolated system is one in which no external force causes energy changes. If only conservative forces do work within an isolated system, the mechanical energy cannot change    (**principle of conservation of mechanical energy**). Thus, $$K_2 + U_2 = K_1 + U_1$$ in which the subscripts refer to different instants during an energy transfer process. This conservation principle can also be written as $$\Delta E_{mec} = \Delta K + \Delta U = 0$$
# 8.3 Reading a PE Curve
- If we know the PE function _U(x)_ in which a one-dimensional force _F(x)_ acts on a particle, we can find the force as $$F(x)=-\frac{dU}{dx}$$
- If _U(x)_ is given on a graph, then at any value of _x_, the force _F(x)_ is the negative of the slope of the curve there and the KE is given by $$K(x)=E_{mec}-U(x)$$
	- \** remember $E_{mec}$ is constant
- A turning point is a point x at which the particle reverses its motion (KE = 0)
- The particle is in equilibrium at points where the slope of the U(x) curve is 0 (F(x) = 0)
	- Neutral Equilibrium: $E_{mec} = U$ --> Particle has no KE --> must be stationary
	- Unstable Equilibrium: If the particle is displaced even slightly, a nonzero force pushes it farther in the same direction, and the particle continues to move
	- Stable Equilibrium: Cannot move on its own b/c it would require neg. KE & if the particle is pushed slightly left or right, a restoring force appears that moves it back
# 8.4 Work Done on a System by an External Force
- Work _W_ is transferred to or from a system by means of an external force acting on th system.
- When more than one force acts on a system, their net work is the transferred energy. 
- When friction is not involved, the work done on the system and the change $\Delta E_{mec}$ are equal: $$W=\Delta E_{mec} = \Delta K + \Delta U $$
- When a kinetic frictional force acts within the system, then the thermal energy $E_{th}$ (associated with the random motion of atoms and molecules) changes: $$\boxed{W = \Delta E_{mec} + E_{th} \qquad \binom{\text{energy statement for a}}{\text{system that involves friction}}}$$
	- The change $\Delta E_{th}$ is related to the magnitude $F_{fk}$ of the frictional force & the magnitude _d_ of the displacement caused by the external force by $$\Delta E_{th} = F_{fk} \ d$$
# 8.5 Conservation of Energy
- **Law of Conservation of Energy**: The total energy _E_ of a system (sum of mech. & internal energies, incl. thermal) can change only by amounts of energy that are transferred to or from the system.
	- $$W = \Delta E = \Delta E_{mec} + \Delta E_{th} + \Delta E_{int}$$
		- where $\Delta E_{mec}$ refers to changes in KE and U & $\Delta E_{int}$ refers to changes in any other type of internal energy of the system
		- If the system is isolated (W = 0), $\Delta E_{mec} + \Delta E_{th} + \Delta E_{int} = 0$ 
	- and $\Delta E_{mec, 2} = \Delta E_{mec, 1} + \Delta E_{th} + \Delta E_{int} = 0$ where subscripts 1 & 2 refer to 2 different instants
	- An external force can change the KE/U of an object w/o doing work on the object/transferring energy to it; the force is responsible for transfers of energy from one type to another inside the object (e.g. an ice-skater pushing away from a railing)
		- Can be simplified b/c the energy still increases as a result of the force, just indirectly
- Power, more generally, due to a force is the rate at which that force transfers energy. If an amount of energy $\Delta E$ is transferred by a force in time $\Delta t$, the average power is $$P_{avg} = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta t}$$
	- Thus, $$P(t) = \frac{dE}{dt}$$
	- On a graph of energy versus time, the power is the slope of the plot at any given time.
