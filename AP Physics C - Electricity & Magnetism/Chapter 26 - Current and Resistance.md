# 26.1 Electric Current
- An electric current _i_ in a conductor is defined by $$i=\frac{dq}{dt}$$where _dq_ is the amount of positive charge that passes in time _dt_. 
- Measured in Amperes = Coulombs/Seconds
- By convention, the direction of electric current is taken as the direction in which positive charge carriers would move even though (normally) only conduction electrons can move
# 26.2 Current Density
- Current _i_ (scalar) is related to current density $\vec{J}$ by $$i = \int\vec{J} \cdot d\vec{A}$$where $d\vec{A}$ is a vector perp. to a surface element of area _dA_ and te integral is taken over any surface cutting across the conductor. The current density has the same direction as the velocity of the moving charges if they are positive and the opposite direction if they are negative.
- When an electric field $\vec{E}$ is established in a conductor, the charge carriers (assumed pos.) acquire a drift speed $v_d$ in the direction of $\vec{E}$
- The drift velocity is related to the current density by $$\vec{J} = (ne)\vec{v}_d$$where _ne_ is the carrier charge density. 
# 26.3 Resistance and Resistivity
- Resistance of a conductor defined as $$R = \frac{V}{i}$$
- Resistivity $\rho$ and conductivity $\sigma$ of a material are related by $$\rho = \frac{1}{\sigma}=\frac{E}{J}$$where E is the mag. of the applied E field and _J_ is the mag. of the current density
	- c.f. R = V \ i  ==> dealing with general terms instead of specific objects
- The electric field and current density are related to the resistivity by $$\vec{E} = \rho\vec{J}$$
- The resistance _R_ of a conducting wire of length _L_ and uniform cross section $$R = \rho\frac{L}{A}$$where A is the cross-sectional area
- Resistivity for most materials changes with temperature; for many materials, including metals, the relation between $\rho$ and _T_ is approx. by the equation $$\rho-\rho_0=\rho_0\alpha(t-T_0)$$where $T_0$ is a reference temperature, $\rho_0$ is the resistivity at $T_0$, and $\alpha$ is the temperature coefficient of resistivity for the material
# 26.4 Ohm's Law
- A given device obeys Ohm's Law if its resistance is independent of the applied potential difference _V_, or if its resistivity is independent of the magnitude and direction of the applied electric field
- The assumption that conduction e- are free to move like the molecules in a gas leads to the expression for the resistivity of a metal: $$\rho=\frac{m}{e^2n\tau}$$where _n_ is the # of free e-/unit volume and $\tau$ is the mean time between the collisions of an e- with the atoms of the metal
- Metals obey Ohm's Law because the mean free time is approx. independent of the magnitude E of any E field applied to a metal
- Ohm's Law is NOT V=IR; V=IR applies to any device, i.e. if you know V and I through a p-n junction you can find its resistance _at that value of V_
# 26.5 Power, Semiconductors, Superconductors
- The power _P_, or rate of energy transfer, in an electrical device across which a potential diff. _V_ is maintained is $$P = iV \quad \text{(electrical energy transfer of all kinds)}$$.
- If the device is a resistor, the power can also be written as $$P=I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R} \quad \text{(EPE to thermal in a device w/ resistance)}$$
- In a resistor, EPE is converted to internal thermal energy via collisions between charge carriers and atoms.
- Semiconductors are materials that have few conduction e- but can become conductors when they are doped with other atoms that contribute charge carriers
- Superconductors are materials that lose all electrical resistance; most such materials require very low tempereatures, but some become superconducting at temperatures as high as room temperature
