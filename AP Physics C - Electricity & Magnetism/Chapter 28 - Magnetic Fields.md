# 28.1 Magnetic Fields and the Definition of B
- When a charged particle moves through a magnetic field $\vec{B}$, a magnetic force acts on the particle as given by $$\vec{F}_B=q(\vec{v}\times\vec{B})$$where _q_ is the particle's charge (sign included) and $\vec{v}$ is the particle's velocity
- The RHR for cross product gives the direction of $\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$; sign of _q_ determines whether $\vec{F}_B$ is in the same direction as $\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$ or in the opp. direction
	- Cross product-- sweep fingers across one vector into the other; thumb points in direction of resultant
- The magnitude is given by $$F_B=|q|vB\sin\phi$$where $\phi$ is the angle between $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{B}$ 
- $F_B$ never has a component parallel to $\vec{v}$; it cannot change the speed (or KE) and only the direction, it only accelerates the particle in this sense
# 28.2 Crossed Fields: Discovery of the Electron
- If a charged particle moves through a region containing both an E and B field, it can be affected by both an electric and magnetic force
- If the fields are perp. ,they are said to be crossed fields
- If the forces are in opposite directions, a particular speed will result in no deflection of the particle
# 28.3 Crossed Fields: The Hall Effect
- When a uniform B field is applied to a conducting strip carrying current _i_, with the field perp. to the direction of the current, a Hall-effect potential diff. _V_ is setup across the strip.
	- The electric force on the charge carriers is then balanced by the magnetic force on them.
	1. B field pushes positive charge carriers to one side
	2. This sets up an E field which opposes this push
	3. Eventually, the two forces reach equilibrium
- The number density _n_ of the charge carriers can then be determined from $$n = \frac{Bi}{Vle}$$where _l_ is the thickness of the strip (parallel to $\vec{B}$)
- When a conductor moves through a uniform magnetic field at speed _v_, the Hall-effect potential difference across it is $$V = vBd$$,where _d_ is the width perp. to both velocity and field. 
# 28.4 A Circulating Charged Particle 
- A charged particle w/ mass _m_ and charge magnitude _|q|_ moving with velocity $\vec{v}$ perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field will travel in a circle
- Applying NSL: $$|q|vB=\frac{mv^2}{r}$$from which we find the radius of the circle to be $$r=\frac{mv}{|q|B}$$
- The frequency, ang. freq., period: $$f=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{T}=\frac{|q|B}{2\pi m}$$
	- All particles w/ the same charge-to-mass ratio have the same period/frequency/ang. vel.
- If the velocity of the particle has a component parallel to the B field, the particle moves in a helical path about field vector $\vec{B}$ 
# 28.5 Cyclotrons and Synchrotrons
- In a cyclotron, charged particles are accelerated by E forces as they circle in a magnetic field
- A synchrotron is needed for particles accelerated to nearly the speed of light

# 28.6 Magnetic Force on a Current-Carrying Wire
- A straight wire carrying a current _i_ in a uniform magnetic field experiences a sideways force $$\vec{F}_B=i\vec{L}\times\vec{B}$$
- The force acting on a current element $id\vec{L}$ in a magnetic field is $$d\vec{F}_B=id\vec{L}\times \vec{B}$$
- The direction of the length vector $\vec L$ or $d \vec L$ is that of the current _i_ 
# 28.7 Torque on a Current Loop
- Various magnetic forces act on the sections of a current-carrying coil lying in a uniform external magnetic field, but the net force is 0. 
- The net torque acting on a coil has magnitude $$\tau = NiAB\sin \theta$$,where N is the # of turns in the coil, A is the area of each turn, _i_ is the current, _B_ is the field magnitude, and $\theta$ is the angle between the magnetic field $\vec{B}$ and the normal vector to the coil $\vec{n}$ 

# 28.8 Magnetic Dipole Moment
- A coil of area A and N turns, carrying current i in a uniform magnetic field will experience a torque $$\vec \tau = \vec{\mu} \times \vec B$$where $\vec \mu$ is the magnetic dipole moment of the coil, with magnitude $\mu = NiA$ and direction given by the RHR
- The orientation energy of a magnetic dipole in a magnetic field is $$U(\theta)=-\vec \mu \cdot \vec B$$
- If an external agent rotates a magnetic dipole from an initial orientation $\theta_0$ to some over orientation $\theta_f$ and the dipole is stationary both initially and finally, the work done on the dipole by the agent is $$W_A = \Delta U = U_f - U_0$$
